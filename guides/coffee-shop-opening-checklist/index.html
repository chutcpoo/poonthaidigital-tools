Opening sequence

Make readiness visible before service begins.

1Review handoff notes and exceptions.
2Prepare equipment, bar and customer areas.
3Confirm stock, POS and first-service readiness.

What should be on a coffee shop opening checklist?

The checklist should cover the small set of recurring tasks that must be complete before normal service starts. The exact tasks depend on your equipment, menu, staffing and approved procedures, but the operating categories can stay consistent from day to day.

1. Read the previous shift handoff first

Start with unresolved information before beginning routine setup. Check notes about low stock, equipment issues, unusual customer orders, maintenance problems, incomplete cleaning or anything the closing team could not finish.

This prevents the opening team from discovering the same problem again after service has already started.

2. Check equipment and work areas

Confirm that equipment needed for service is available and ready according to your shop's approved procedures. Then make sure the bar and front counter are organized so the first orders can move through the normal workflow.

  • Review equipment condition and any maintenance notes.
  • Prepare the bar, counter and service stations.
  • Confirm cups, lids, napkins and other frequently used service supplies are stocked.
  • Make sure customer-facing areas are ready for opening.

Do not use a generic checklist as a substitute for manufacturer instructions, food-safety procedures or workplace requirements.

3. Review the stock that can stop service

An opening checklist does not need to become a full inventory count. Focus on the items that could interrupt service if they are unexpectedly low. If a shortage needs purchasing action, move it into a separate inventory or reorder workflow instead of burying it inside the opening list.

4. Confirm the front counter and POS workflow

The opening routine should make it clear who verifies the register or point-of-sale setup and what “ready” means for the shop. Keep cash-handling details aligned with the business's approved controls rather than copying a generic procedure from the internet.

5. Define first-service readiness

The checklist should end with a short readiness check. A useful final question is not “Did we complete the checklist?” but “Can the team handle the first normal customer order without stopping to fix a setup problem?”

Opening checklist goal: unresolved issue → assigned owner or handoff note; routine task → visibly complete before service.

Example daily opening structure

HandoffRead shortages, equipment issues and unfinished work.
Bar & equipmentPrepare required work areas and verify service equipment.
Front counterSet up the customer-facing area and normal order flow.
StockCheck high-use items that could stop service.
Cash / POSFollow the shop's approved opening control procedure.
Ready to openConfirm exceptions have an owner before the first customer.

How long should an opening checklist be?

Long enough to protect the important routine, short enough that the team will actually use it. Move weekly deep-clean tasks, full inventory counts and maintenance schedules into separate workflows instead of adding everything to one daily opening list.

Three mistakes that make daily checklists fail

  1. Tasks are vague. “Set up bar” may mean something different to every person. Define the useful completion state.
  2. No exception path exists. A problem should become a named handoff or action, not disappear because the list was checked off.
  3. Every frequency is mixed together. Daily opening, weekly cleaning and monthly maintenance are different operating rhythms.
